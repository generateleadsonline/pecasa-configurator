Garden Room Cost and Prices: What Actually Determines the Quote?
Garden room cost is shaped by foundations, SIP structure, glazing, cladding, roofing, interior finish, electrics, AC Installation, plumbing, access and site works. Garden room prices should therefore be compared using a like-for-like inclusions schedule, not a headline figure.
Why a headline price is rarely enough
Two garden rooms with identical external dimensions can have very different final costs. One quotation may cover an insulated shell; another may include foundations, Tape-and-joint internal finish, flooring, glazing, sockets, lighting, AC Installation and finished cladding. A price has meaning only when the inclusions and assumptions are clear.
For that reason, the best buying question is not simply ‘how much per square metre?’. It is ‘what finished building will I have for this total, and what will I still need to arrange separately?’. That distinction prevents apparently cheap quotations becoming more expensive once essential items are added.
The largest cost drivers
Size affects structure, labour and finishes, but geometry also matters. Extensive glazing, skylights, corner openings, internal partitions and complex roof or façade details can increase both material and installation requirements. Cladding and internal finish choices can move the budget further.
Services are another major variable. A basic office with lighting and sockets is different from a gym with AC Installation or a multi-purpose room with plumbing. Water, drainage, hot water, ventilation and specialist electrical loads should be priced against the real site.
Foundation, ground and access
The ground beneath the room and the route into the garden influence cost even though they disappear from the finished photographs. Slopes, trees, made ground, existing patios, restricted side access and long carrying routes can affect foundation design and logistics.
Pecasa commonly uses ground screw foundations on suitable projects and also provides site surveys and testing. A quotation should state what ground preparation, levelling and access assumptions have been made so the foundation allowance can be understood.

Why London quotations may differ
Urban sites can involve no side access, controlled parking, limited storage, narrow routes through the house and close boundaries. These constraints do not automatically make every project expensive, but they can affect labour, handling and the sequence of installation.
The most realistic budget is produced when the initial design is combined with site information. Pecasa’s 3D configurator can provide an indicative price and design before the team refines the proposal around access, ground and specification.
Planning, professional fees and allowances
Some garden rooms can proceed under permitted development while others need planning input. A Lawful Development Certificate, planning application, structural engineering, Building Control or drainage design may create additional professional or statutory costs. These should be identified separately from the construction price where they apply.
Do not publish or rely on old application-fee figures without checking them. Statutory and local authority fees change, so the current Planning Portal, GOV.UK and local council information should be used at the time of application.
How to compare quotations fairly
Create an inclusions schedule with the foundation, wall and roof system, roof covering, cladding, glazing, Tape-and-joint internal finish, flooring, skirting, sockets, lighting, heating or cooling, data, plumbing, waste removal, decorating, external making-good and planning support. Mark each line as included, optional or excluded.
The most reliable comparison is an inclusions schedule covering foundations, shell, glazing, cladding, internal finish, services, site works, approvals and exclusions.
How to control cost without undermining quality
If the budget is tight, simplify geometry and non-essential finishes before cutting the building envelope or foundation. A straightforward footprint and rational glazing arrangement can reduce cost while preserving the parts that determine long-term performance.
Decide what must be ready on day one. Built-in cabinetry, premium landscaping or specialist AV equipment can often be added later, whereas hidden electrical provision, drainage routes and structural openings are easier to plan during construction.

Questions to resolve before approval
Confirm the final external and internal dimensions, finished floor level, glazing orientation, access route, service connections and any landscaping that must remain protected. If there are trees, inspection chambers, retaining walls or known buried services near the proposed work, flag them before the foundation or service design is finalised.
Ask the proposal to distinguish fixed inclusions from optional items and assumptions. A detailed scope reduces misunderstandings and makes later changes easier to price and approve.
Think about long-term use
Design decisions should reflect how the space or system may be used in several years, not only the immediate requirement. Extra service capacity, adaptable layouts, maintainable connections and clear documentation can preserve options without materially changing the first-day appearance.
Long-term value is usually driven by the fundamentals: a suitable foundation, sound structural design, weather protection, thermal performance where relevant, competent installation and straightforward maintenance. Decorative features are easier to change later.
